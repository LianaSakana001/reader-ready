# Scientific manuscripts

Use this guidance for drafting and revising research articles, abstracts, reviews, theses, and scientific figure legends. Follow the target journal and disciplinary conventions supplied by the user. Read the corresponding Chinese guide only if needed; the editorial rules are equivalent.

## Register and authorial position

Write precise academic prose. Use field-standard terminology consistently. Passive voice is appropriate when the procedure or result is central; first-person plural is appropriate when the journal and authors use it. Neither is a universal requirement. Avoid literary imagery, staged suspense, casual questions, emotional reactions, personal anecdotes, and promotional claims.

Retain meaningful transitions, parallel descriptions of comparable experiments, structured abstracts, and conventional section headings. Do not replace repeated technical terms with stylistic synonyms that change or blur the concept. Edit sentences for clarity, not to meet a rhythm, punctuation, or sentence-length quota.

## A compelling research argument

Make the scientific contribution easy to see. Connect the research problem to the unresolved question, the approach, the decisive findings, and what those findings change. Use this as a reasoning aid, not a mandatory paragraph template. A descriptive study, a negative result, or a method can each have a strong organizing question. Do not invent a mechanism to make the story fit.

When structure is within scope, order results and figures by their role in that argument rather than by the chronology of the analysis. State the supported advance explicitly and proportionately. Choose informative titles and topic sentences; explain why a result matters using supplied evidence and literature. Avoid both generic hype and a flat inventory of everything performed.

Keep the emphasis purposeful without suppressing evidence that changes the main interpretation. A useful narrative can end on the contribution and its implications rather than introducing a new, generic list of shortcomings. A real response to a reviewer should still answer the actual question directly.

## Avoid defensive writing

State the finding at the strength the evidence supports. Do not automatically append “cannot prove,” “does not establish,” or “should be interpreted with caution” to each result. Wording such as “was associated with” can already express the appropriate claim. Distinguish what the paper must report from what each sentence needs to say.

Use author-facing constraints such as “no causal evidence” or “do not imply a mechanism” to choose accurate wording; do not automatically turn them into additional manuscript sentences. When an association is already stated accurately and no causal interpretation is implied, a separate denial of causality usually adds no information. Keep concrete study findings and material limitations, but distinguish them from instructions about how to write.

For an apparent caveat, decide whether it changes the reader's interpretation, states a necessary condition, or answers an actual issue. Keep that information where needed. If it repeats a boundary already clear from the claim, pre-empts a hypothetical criticism, or apologizes for the study's scope, omit the repetition or state the relevant scope directly. Do not replace it with another disclaimer.

Place material limitations where readers need them, often in Methods or Discussion, without interrupting every result. Keep a qualification adjacent when moving it would make that claim misleading; respect abstract and reporting requirements. Avoid blanket bans and quotas for qualifiers. Do not invent reasons for weak results, redefine prespecified outcomes to favor a story, or remove inconvenient findings that bear on the claim.

Example: a Results sentence reports an observational association; the study design and relevant limitations are already explained.

- Draft: `X was associated with Y. We must emphasize that this cannot prove causality and should not be overinterpreted.`
- Revision: `X was associated with Y.`

The revised sentence makes no causal claim. If the surrounding passage instead implies a mechanism, revise that implication or retain the specific limitation it needs.

## Evidence before phrasing

When drafting, use the supplied results, methods, and sources. When editing, preserve supported claims and their references. Distinguish observation, interpretation, hypothesis, and proposed future work. Do not invent experiments, participant characteristics, statistical tests, references, author contributions, or reasons for a finding.

Keep numerical values, denominators, units, effect sizes, confidence intervals, P values, correction methods, and comparison directions as supplied. Do not treat the number of cells as the number of independent biological replicates. Preserve the distinction between descriptive and inferential results. A nonsignificant result does not establish equivalence or absence of an effect.

Retain evidence-based qualifiers and limitations. “May,” “suggests,” “associated with,” and “within this cohort” can carry essential meaning. Remove duplicated hedging only when the uncertainty remains intact. Do not promote association to causation, exploratory findings to confirmation, or submission to acceptance.

If a source or result is missing, ask for what is needed or keep the gap in author-facing discussion. A requested working draft may contain clearly identified placeholders, but cannot be described as submission-ready while they remain. Preserve required methodological and AI-use disclosures when applicable to the supplied submission requirements; remove irrelevant chat or production narration.

## Match the section's job

| Section | Editorial focus |
| --- | --- |
| Abstract | State the question, approach, main supported findings, and scoped conclusion. Do not add results absent from the manuscript. |
| Introduction | Explain the established problem, specific gap, and study objective; retain the literature needed to support that progression. |
| Methods | Preserve enough procedural detail for the stated study and reporting requirements. Software versions, parameters, exclusions, and QC can be essential here. |
| Results | State comparisons and findings with the supplied quantitative evidence. Keep interpretation proportionate and separate when the journal requires it. |
| Discussion | Relate findings to evidence and prior work; keep alternative explanations, limitations, and the boundary of generalization. |
| Figures and legends | Preserve panel mappings, definitions, sample units, group labels, test information, scale bars, and abbreviations needed to interpret the figure. Remove irrelevant working names. |

## Examples

Evidence: an observational analysis reported an association between X and Y; causality was not established.

- Draft: `Our groundbreaking analysis unveils a fascinating causal landscape between X and Y.`
- Revision: `X was associated with Y in the observational analysis.`
- If causal uncertainty is material to the passage, retain or state that it was not established. Do not invent a mechanism or numerical estimate.

Clear methods prose can stay unchanged:

> Samples were processed in randomized order. Analysts were blinded to group allocation.

A legitimate limitation can stay formal:

> The limited number of donors may reduce the precision of the estimated group difference.

In Chinese, favor precise sentences such as “本研究比较了两组的细胞组成” over generic stage-setting or literary imagery. In English, favor established scientific phrasing over colloquial substitutes such as “we took a look” or “the cells told a story.”
